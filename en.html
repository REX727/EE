Stage 1 / Circuit fundamentals

01Circuits, energy and signals

Draw separate power and signal diagrams, and explain the role of each wire.

You will need: A breadboard, multimeter and basic components; paper calculations are also useful.

Core concepts

Start with a complete loop

Current needs a closed path. A source supplies energy; a load converts it into light, heat or motion. Electric charge is not used up after passing through an LED. A schematic shows electrical connections, not physical positions on your desk.

Voltage needs a reference

A point described as 3.3 V is 3.3 V relative to another point. GND is normally the chosen reference and return network; it is not necessarily earth. Ordinary non-isolated GPIO connections need a common reference. Sharing ground does not mean connecting every positive supply together.

Control wires and power wires

A microcontroller provides control signals; a driver handles load current. Replacing an LED with a motor requires more than changing the load. Motor power and switching interference need appropriate circuits.

Use your hardware as examples

The Pi 5 runs Linux and higher-level software. The ESP32-C3 handles I/O, and Arduino is an ecosystem that can program it. The corresponding official Yahboom four-channel board includes an STM32 for motors and encoders. Verify your actual revision before relying on this; adding an ESP32 to a working car is optional.

Worked example

For a button-controlled car, separate two paths: button → controller → driver carries information; battery → driver → motor → battery carries energy. The button does not need to carry the current of all the motors.

Text wiring / process reference
Signal: button → ESP32 / Pi → driver
Power: battery + → driver → motor → return → battery −

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Disconnect power and list the exact boards and battery models. Mark unknown specifications as unconfirmed.
  2. Back up the working car software and settings before changing anything.
  3. Draw one power diagram and one signal diagram.
  4. Trace a complete LED or motor circuit, including every ground connection.
  5. Explain which disconnected wire would remove power and which would only remove commands.

What should you keep?

A hardware list, power diagram and signal diagram, with unknown voltages clearly marked.

When something goes wrong

If the result differs from your prediction, disconnect power and check the wiring, units and component specifications. Change one thing at a time, then repeat the measurement.

Stage 1 / Circuit fundamentals

02Voltage, current, resistance and power

Choose an LED resistor using Ohm’s law and interpret a power supply label.

You will need: A red LED, 330 Ω and 1 kΩ resistors, a 3.3 V supply and a multimeter.

Core concepts

Get the units right

V measures voltage, A current, Ω resistance and W power. 1 mA = 0.001 A; 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω. For a resistor, V = IR. Power is P = VI; for a resistor it is also I²R or V²/R.

A current rating describes capability

A 5 V / 3 A supply can provide that current under its specified conditions. It does not force 3 A into every load. Incorrect voltage, a short circuit or excessive demand can still cause failure.

An LED needs current limiting

An LED is not a fixed resistor. Its forward voltage Vf depends on colour, current and temperature. A series resistor limits current. GPIO pins have their own current limits, so start with a low-current example.

Calculate power to understand heat

Resistors have power ratings. Regulators and drivers also dissipate heat. Maximum ratings depend on operating conditions; allow margin instead of running continuously at the limit.

Worked example

With 3.3 V, a red LED with an assumed Vf of 2.0 V, and a target of 4 mA: R = (3.3 − 2.0) / 0.004 = 325 Ω. Choose 330 Ω. The resistor dissipates about 0.005 W. Using 1 kΩ instead gives approximately 1.3 mA.

Assumes 3.3 V and LED Vf = 2.0 V. This is a calculation model.

Text wiring / process reference
3V3 ── 330 Ω ── LED (anode → cathode) ── GND
Measure resistor voltage VR; estimate I = VR / R.

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Calculate the expected current with 330 Ω and 1 kΩ. Write down your assumed LED forward voltage.
  2. If you have not used a multimeter, do the calculations now and return after Unit 04.
  3. With power off, connect 3V3 → resistor → LED → GND. Check polarity and breadboard connections.
  4. Power the board by USB and measure the resistor and LED voltages. Never place a meter in current mode across a supply.
  5. Disconnect power before changing the resistor. Repeat the measurements and compare the sum of the voltage drops with the supply.

What should you keep?

A comparison of predicted current, measured voltage drops, calculated current and brightness for both resistors.

When something goes wrong

If the result differs from your prediction, disconnect power and check the wiring, units and component specifications. Change one thing at a time, then repeat the measurement.

03Series, parallel and voltage dividers

Predict branch currents and node voltages, and recognise a divider’s limits.

You will need: Three 10 kΩ resistors, a multimeter and a 3.3 V supply.

Core concepts

Series components share current

Current is the same along a path with no branches. Series resistances add. Around a complete loop, voltage rises and drops sum to zero: an intuitive form of Kirchhoff’s voltage law.

Parallel components share voltage

Components connected to the same two nodes have the same voltage. Current divides between branches; total current entering a node equals total current leaving it. Parallel resistance follows 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2. Two equal resistors in parallel have half the resistance of one.

The divider equation has conditions

For an upper R1 and lower R2, the unloaded output is Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2), measured relative to ground. Adding a load RL makes the lower resistance R2 in parallel with RL, usually reducing Vout.

Scaling a signal is not powering a device

A divider can be useful for low-current measurements. It is not a regulator for an ESP32 or motor. ADC connections also require checks of voltage limits, source impedance, sampling, calibration and protection.

Worked example

With 3.3 V and two 10 kΩ resistors, unloaded Vout is 1.65 V. Add a 10 kΩ load to ground: the lower equivalent is 5 kΩ, so Vout = 3.3 × 5 / (10 + 5) = 1.10 V.

Text wiring / process reference
3V3 ── R1 ──●── R2 ── GND
             └── RL ── GND
             Vout relative to GND

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Calculate 1 kΩ + 2 kΩ in series, and two 10 kΩ resistors in parallel.
  2. With power off, build a divider from two 10 kΩ resistors. Leave ESP32 GPIO disconnected.
  3. Apply power and measure Vin and Vout. Note the effect of resistor tolerance.
  4. Disconnect power, add the third 10 kΩ resistor as a load, and measure again.
  5. Use the results to explain why a divider’s output falls under a heavy load.

What should you keep?

Two circuit diagrams and a table comparing unloaded and loaded calculations with measurements.

Stage 1 / Circuit fundamentals

04Measurements and schematics

Use a multimeter safely and turn a schematic into actual wiring.

You will need: A breadboard, multimeter and basic components; paper calculations are also useful.

Core concepts

Wires represent nodes

Connected wires belong to the same electrical node. Crossing lines may or may not connect; check the junction symbol. Ground symbols normally identify a shared net. Separate power, load and control sections when reading a drawing.

Measure voltage in parallel

Use the COM and V/Ω sockets and select DC voltage. Measure between two points, using ground as the reference where appropriate. Slipping probes can short neighbouring pins; attach suitable test leads before powering narrow connectors.

Measure resistance with power removed

Disconnect the battery and USB before measuring resistance or continuity. Parallel paths on a board can change the reading. A continuity beep does not prove zero resistance; a charging capacitor may briefly cause a beep.

Measure current in series

Current mode has low resistance and must be inserted into the load path. Use the correct socket, range and fuse. Beginners can infer current from a resistor’s voltage drop; high motor-current measurements are not required. A multimeter may miss brief voltage dips.

Worked example

If an LED does not light, check supply voltage, then disconnect power and inspect the breadboard connections, LED direction and resistor. Do not bypass the resistor to test it. A 1.2 V drop across 330 Ω implies about 3.64 mA.

Text wiring / process reference
Voltage: meter across the load (parallel)
Current: source → meter → load (series)
Resistance / continuity: all power disconnected

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. On an unpowered breadboard, identify connected rows and any breaks in the power rails.
  2. Draw an LED schematic and build it from the drawing.
  3. Check the probe sockets and mode. Measure 3V3 and the resistor and LED voltage drops.
  4. With power off, disconnect one LED branch wire. Diagnose the open circuit systematically, then restore it.
  5. Record the range and readings. Extra display digits do not automatically mean greater accuracy.

What should you keep?

Your schematic, wiring photograph and a record of diagnosing an open circuit.

When something goes wrong

If the result differs from your prediction, disconnect power and check the wiring, units and component specifications. Change one thing at a time, then repeat the measurement.

Stage 2 / Components & power

05Capacitors and inductors

Understand energy storage, RC behaviour, decoupling and current paths.

You will need: A 10 kΩ resistor, a 100 µF capacitor with a suitable voltage rating, and a multimeter.

Core concepts

Capacitors oppose sudden voltage changes

Capacitor current is I = C × dV/dt. An RC circuit does not instantly reach its final voltage. Its time constant is τ = RC; a simple first-order charging circuit reaches about 63% after one τ. Electrolytic capacitors have polarity and voltage ratings.

Decoupling and bulk capacitance

A capacitor close to a chip supplies short local demands. Bulk capacitance helps with larger temporary changes at the power input. Placement, current loops and component properties matter. A capacitor cannot fix a continuous power shortage.

Inductors oppose sudden current changes

V = L × dI/dt. Motors and relays contain coils. Suddenly interrupting their current can generate high voltage; stored energy needs an appropriate recirculation or absorption path. This is one reason not to connect motors directly to GPIO.

Two ways to store energy

Capacitors store energy in an electric field: E = ½CV². Inductors use a magnetic field: E = ½LI². Understand these relationships before studying filters and switching supplies in more depth.

Worked example

10 kΩ and 100 µF give τ ≈ 1 second. Charging from 3.3 V reaches about 2.1 V after one second and nearly the final value after five seconds. A multimeter may show this slow change, but tolerance and display update rate affect the observations.

Text wiring / process reference
3V3 ── 10 kΩ ──●── capacitor ── GND
                └── measure voltage to GND

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Verify the 100 µF capacitor’s polarity and a voltage rating above the supply voltage.
  2. Build the circuit with power off. Discharge through a resistor and check that voltage is near zero.
  3. Apply 3.3 V and record capacitor voltage at approximately 1, 2 and 5 seconds.
  4. Disconnect power and discharge through 10 kΩ. Never use a metal short to discharge it.
  5. Sketch charging and discharging curves. Explain why this slow RC circuit should not power an ESP32.

What should you keep?

A labelled RC circuit and a measured or predicted charging and discharging curve.

Stage 2 / Components & power

06Diodes, transistors and MOSFETs

Understand electronic switches and separate the control side from the load side.

You will need: A breadboard, multimeter and basic components; paper calculations are also useful.

Core concepts

A diode is not a perfect one-way valve

Diodes have a forward voltage drop, current and thermal limits, and a reverse-voltage rating. Choose a flyback diode for the load, switching speed and stored energy. Appearance alone does not make a signal diode interchangeable with a power diode.

BJT and MOSFET control

A BJT requires base current. A MOSFET’s conduction depends on gate-to-source voltage, and switching requires charging and discharging its gate. The gate is not a motor supply input. VGS(th) does not prove low-resistance operation at 3.3 V; check guaranteed RDS(on) at the relevant VGS.

An N-channel low-side switch

Source connects to ground, drain to the load’s negative terminal, and the load’s positive terminal to its supply. The controller shares ground. A gate pull-down can keep the switch off during reset. A series gate resistor affects switching; its value depends on the design.

Start with a small non-inductive load

Use an LED and resistor first. Inductive loads require appropriate energy paths. A diode arrangement for a one-direction coil switch cannot be copied blindly across a bidirectional H-bridge.

Worked example

At 0.5 A, a MOSFET with RDS(on) = 0.1 Ω at the actual gate voltage dissipates I²R = 0.025 W. If incomplete turn-on gives 2 Ω instead, dissipation becomes 0.5 W: twenty times higher.

Text wiring / process reference
Load supply + → LED + resistor → Drain
GPIO → suitable gate circuit → Gate
Common GND ─────────────────→ Source

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Choose a documented MOSFET or module suitable for a 3.3 V gate signal. Without one, work on paper first.
  2. Identify D, G and S and the test conditions for RDS(on) in the datasheet.
  3. Build a low-side switch for a small LED load with power disconnected. Do not attach a motor.
  4. After Unit 09, use GPIO to switch it and observe its state during reset.
  5. Draw the additional protection and current requirements for a coil load, without powering that version yet.

What should you keep?

A pin-labelled switch diagram, the relevant datasheet conditions and a reset-state observation.

Stage 2 / Components & power

07Power supplies and regulators

Build a power budget and understand batteries, regulators and heat.

You will need: A breadboard, multimeter and basic components; paper calculations are also useful.

Core concepts

Battery voltage changes

Check chemistry, series cell count, full-charge voltage and the permitted minimum voltage. Capacity in mAh describes charge, not maximum current capability. Wh ≈ nominal V × Ah is more useful for comparing stored energy.

LDO and buck regulators

An LDO dissipates the voltage difference as heat: approximately P = (Vin − Vout)I. A buck converter switches energy through storage components to reduce voltage. Its efficiency depends on conditions, and it cannot maintain a higher output from an arbitrarily low input.

Add power across different voltage rails

Only currents referred to the same supply node can be added directly. A 5 V, 2 A load needs 10 W. At 7.4 V and an assumed 90% efficiency, it draws approximately 1.50 A from the battery, before other loads and transients.

USB and external power

Do not connect USB and an external 5 V source simultaneously on an undocumented board; backfeeding may occur. Check the power path first. Your exact power expansion board is unconfirmed, so a “Pi power” label does not establish a continuous 5 A rating.

Worked example

An LDO converting 12 V to 3.3 V at 0.2 A dissipates 1.74 W. With an assumed 90%-efficient buck, the same 0.66 W output needs about 0.733 W input and loses about 0.073 W. These are calculations, not guarantees for an arbitrary module.

Text wiring / process reference
Battery → motor branch
        → buck regulator → logic / Pi branch
Refer each branch’s power back to the battery.

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. List each rail’s voltage and average and peak current, including the source of each number.
  2. Calculate output power, then estimate battery-side demand using stated efficiency assumptions.
  3. Check input range, continuous capability, cooling conditions and the USB power path.
  4. On a verified supply, measure idle and small-load voltages. Do not perform a stall test.
  5. Label every number as measured, specified or assumed rather than treating all as equally certain.

What should you keep?

A power budget with sources, assumptions, voltage ranges and thermal estimates.

If the result differs from your prediction, disconnect power and check the wiring, units and component specifications. Change one thing at a time, then repeat the measurement.

Stage 2 / Components & power

08Datasheets and logic-level compatibility

Decide whether devices can connect directly or need an interface.

You will need: A breadboard, multimeter and basic components; paper calculations are also useful.

Core concepts

Start with recommended operating conditions

Absolute maximum ratings are limits, not normal operating targets. Check pinout, power, logic thresholds, output type, reference circuits and layout requirements. A product title is not a datasheet.

Logic levels are voltage ranges

A HIGH output is not always exactly the supply voltage. Compare sender VOH/VOL with receiver VIH/VIL, and stay inside input voltage limits. A 3.3 V controller cannot automatically drive every 5 V device.

Push-pull and open-drain outputs

Push-pull actively drives high and low. Open-drain drives low and relies on a pull-up for high. I²C commonly uses open-drain; UART is usually push-pull. An arbitrary bidirectional level-shifter module is not suitable for every fast interface.

Write a wiring contract

For each connection, record direction, voltage, default state, interface and pin. Matching connector pin counts do not imply matching protocols. ESP32-C3 boot-strapping and USB pins also need special consideration.

Worked example

A sensor powered from 5 V with a 5 V push-pull output must not connect directly to an ESP32 input. An open-drain output might use a 3.3 V pull-up only if the device permits it and the board does not already pull the line to 5 V. Verify the circuit.

Text wiring / process reference
Sender output range → receiver thresholds
Also check: input maximum, ground, direction and default state.

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Find official documentation or a reliable schematic for a module you plan to use.
  2. List VCC, GND, signal pins and output types.
  3. Compare the controller’s input limits and logic thresholds with the module’s specifications.
  4. Inspect on-board pull-ups and boot-related pins. Do not label unknowns as compatible.
  5. Draw one direct connection and one connection requiring conversion. Explain each decision.

What should you keep?

A compatibility table and two annotated connection diagrams.

If the result differs from your prediction, disconnect power and check the wiring, units and component specifications. Change one thing at a time, then repeat the measurement.

Stage 3 / ESP32 & programming

09Your board and development environment

Upload a program, read serial output and distinguish build and upload problems.

You will need: An ESP32-C3 SuperMini, a data-capable USB cable and a computer.

Core concepts

A C3 SuperMini is a suitable starting point

It can handle most individual experiments here. “SuperMini” does not identify one fixed board design; save its pinout and a photo. GPIO numbers are not physical header positions. Use Unit 26 to choose another controller when you need more resources.

Installation and versions

Install Espressif’s esp32 package through Boards Manager in Arduino IDE 2.x and record the version. The LEDC servo example uses Arduino-ESP32 3.x. Older tutorials may use different APIs.

Board selection and USB

Follow the vendor’s board instructions. If no specific option exists, documentation may direct you to ESP32C3 Dev Module; verify flash and native USB CDC settings. The USB cable must support data. Port names may change after reconnecting.

Separate three types of failure

For compilation errors, read the first meaningful error. For upload failures, check the cable, board, port and download mode. For successful uploads with no output, check USB CDC, the serial port and program. Change one setting at a time.

Worked example

Upload a program that only prints HELLO before attaching any external load. Once the counter works, add an LED. This keeps USB problems separate from wiring problems.

Text wiring / process reference
IDE → compile → upload → ESP32
ESP32 → USB serial → Serial Monitor

Conceptual reference; follow your actual module documentation for wiring.

Try it yourself

  1. Install the IDE and esp32 package. Record the version and board options.
  2. Connect only USB, paste the example and select Verify.
  3. Choose the correct port, upload and open Serial Monitor at 115200.
  4. If necessary, enter download mode using the board’s instructions. A common sequence is holding BOOT, pressing and releasing RESET, then releasing BOOT.
  5. Restart the board and check that the count starts again. Save the working minimal program.

Code example

Inputs and outputs have different jobs

Debouncing is a timing decision

The example starts with 30 ms: update the timestamp whenever the raw input changes, and accept a new stable state only after 30 ms. This is an experimental starting point, not a universal switch specification.

12Analog input, PWM and timing

Core concepts

ADC converts voltage to numbers

PWM switches rapidly

Limit the analog input range

Connect 3V3 through a fixed 10 kΩ resistor to the high end of a 10 kΩ potentiometer, its low end to ground and its wiper to GPIO0. Maximum output is approximately 1.65 V. Measure it first and confirm GPIO0 is an available ADC input on your board.

Schedule work independently

Update control every 10 ms and report every 500 ms instead of blocking everything with a long delay. Investigate clipping, noise and mapping errors separately.

Worked example

At 1 kHz, the PWM period is 1 ms. At 25% duty cycle, HIGH lasts 0.25 ms. A 12-bit ADC has 4096 codes, but attenuation, calibration and noise still affect voltage accuracy.

13Sensors and data quality

Decide whether measurements are trustworthy and apply simple calibration and filtering.

You will need: A breadboard, multimeter and basic components; paper calculations are also useful.

Core concepts

Sensors measure particular quantities

Light, temperature, distance and inertial sensors measure different physical quantities. Range, resolution, accuracy, update rate and latency are distinct specifications. An accelerometer’s reading includes gravity; it is not simply vehicle speed.

Separate bias from noise

A fixed offset is bias; random fluctuation is one form of noise; a slowly changing error is drift. Averaging can reduce some random noise but cannot automatically correct scale errors or clipping.

Two-point calibration

If raw reading r and actual value x are approximately linear, two known points give x = a r + b. Use the relationship only over a justified range. Two points cannot correct every nonlinearity, and the reference instrument also has error.

Filtering has a cost

An N-point moving average smooths data but adds delay. Exponential averaging uses y = αx + (1 − α)y_previous. Smaller α gives a steadier but slower response. Detect outliers, disconnections and invalid codes instead of averaging them as valid measurements.

Worked example

If actual values 0 and 100 produce readings 10 and 110, x = r − 10. If actual 100 instead produces 210, use a = 100 / (210 − 10) = 0.5 and b = −5.

Core concepts

UART and USB are different

UART uses TX/RX signals with matching baud rate, format and logic levels. USB has different electrical and protocol layers. A USB serial interface lets software work with a serial-like port; USB D+/D− are not UART TX/RX.

Bare UART wiring

Connect TX to the other device’s RX, RX to TX, and share ground after confirming compatible logic levels. Do not join two TX outputs. TTL UART is electrically different from RS-232 and RS-485 despite all being serial interfaces.

Protocols need boundaries and error handling

Here, ON, OFF and STATUS end with LF; CR is ignored, so CRLF also works. Unknown commands return an error and overlong lines are discarded. A fixed buffer prevents unlimited accumulation. Define command units and permitted ranges.

Timeout is a system behaviour

The example turns an LED off after one second without a new ON. STATUS does not refresh this timer. This is not a motor emergency stop. If a Pi crashes, it cannot reliably send OFF; loss-of-command handling belongs at the execution end.

Worked example

Send ON plus a newline: the board replies OK ON and lights the LED. Stop refreshing ON and it goes off after about one second. Repeated STATUS commands only report the button and do not keep the LED on.

    active=true; lastOn=millis(); Serial.println("OK ON");
  } else if (!strcmp(s,"OFF")) {
    active=false; Serial.println("OK OFF");
  } else if (!strcmp(s,"STATUS")) {
    Serial.print("BUTTON=");
    Serial.println(digitalRead(BUTTON_PIN)==LOW ? 1 : 0);
  } else { Serial.println("ERR COMMAND"); }
}
void setup() {
  pinMode(LED_PIN,OUTPUT); digitalWrite(LED_PIN,LOW);
  pinMode(BUTTON_PIN,INPUT_PULLUP); Serial.begin(115200);
}
void loop() {
  while (Serial.available()) {
    char c=Serial.read();
    if (c=='\r') continue;
    if (c=='\n') {
      if (dropping) Serial.println("ERR TOO_LONG");
      else { buffer[used]='\0'; command(buffer); }
      used=0; dropping=false;
    } else if (!dropping) {
      if (used < sizeof(buffer)-1) buffer[used++]=c;
      else { dropping=true; used=0; }
    }

15I²C and SPI

Understand two peripheral interfaces and connect sensors from their documentation.

You will need: An ESP32 and an optional, verified 3.3 V-compatible I²C module.

Core concepts

I²C selects devices by address

SDA carries data and SCL carries clock. Devices need ground and suitable pull-ups. Two devices with the same address may require an address change or multiplexer. Finding an address does not prove the data format is correct.

Pull-ups and wiring length

Modules may already contain pull-ups; several in parallel reduce resistance. Weak pull-ups can cause slow rising edges, while strong ones increase LOW-state current. Check voltage, use short wires and start at a documented conservative speed such as 100 kHz.

SPI uses chip select

Typical signals are SCLK, MOSI, MISO and a separate CS for each device. Clock polarity, phase, bit order and speed must match. Devices can share some lines only if unselected outputs do not interfere with the bus.

Choose an interface for its use

I²C uses fewer wires and suits multiple sensors. SPI is common for higher data rates but needs more wiring and chip selects. Neither is a universal solution for long robot wiring runs.

Worked example

Two modules each carrying 4.7 kΩ pull-ups produce an equivalent 2.35 kΩ. “Supports I²C” is not enough: high-level voltage, addresses and bus loading must also be compatible.

Core concepts

Describe the task first

Torque and speed

For a perpendicular force, τ = F × r. Mechanical power is P = τ × ω. A gearbox reduces speed and increases output torque but introduces loss, backlash and load limits. A larger wheel gives less ground force at the same shaft torque.

Acceleration and holding matter

Different actuators suit different jobs

Brushed DC motors with H-bridges suit wheels; positioning needs feedback. RC servos integrate control; smart servos add packet commands and status. Distinguish pulse-position commands from digital packets and plan compatible power and communication.

You will need: An optional RC servo with compatible supply; AX-18A, U2D2 and its supply are a separate optional branch.

Core concepts

An RC servo’s signal wire carries a command

Common positional RC servos use repeated pulse width to set a target, unlike high-frequency DC motor power PWM. 1.5 ms centre pulses and 50 Hz are common examples, but check voltage, frequency, pulse range and travel for the model. A continuous-rotation servo usually maps pulse width to speed/direction instead of position.

Power and mechanical travel

An internal motor can draw substantial startup or obstruction current. Use a correctly rated load supply and common ground, not a GPIO or tiny 3V3 regulator. Remove linkages or clear the travel path for first motion. Test a small range without driving into end stops.

Smart servos use data

An ID selects a device, commands access parameters, and replies can report position or status. Match the protocol, control table and electrical interface. The AX-18A example uses Protocol 1.0 and half-duplex TTL, with 9–12 V supply and 11.1 V recommended. 20Stepper motors

Understand STEP/DIR, current limits, missed steps and homing, then test one free shaft conservatively.

You will need: A bipolar stepper, compatible STEP/DIR driver, suitable power and capacitors; a limit switch is optional for the first free-shaft test.

Core concepts

Pulses and shaft angle

A bipolar stepper usually has two coils driven in sequence. A 1.8° full step gives 200 steps per turn. At 1/16 microstepping, one commanded revolution uses 3200 pulses. Microsteps improve smoothness and command resolution, but do not guarantee a proportional increase in mechanical accuracy.

Use a compatible current-limiting driver

Set coil current using the actual board and sense-resistor values. Different A4988 clone boards may use different Vref relationships. The coil’s rated voltage is not necessarily the required supply voltage for a chopper driver; observe driver input limits and motor current and temperature limits.

Verify wiring and power

With power off, use resistance mode to identify each coil pair. Do not connect or disconnect motor wires while powered. On the referenced Pololu A4988 board, logic VDD and VMOT are separate, and nearby decoupling and bulk capacitance help handle spikes. Follow the documentation for your own board.

Open-loop position can be wrong

Excessive acceleration, load or unsuitable current can cause missed steps even while software believes the move is complete. Startup position is normally unknown. Homing uses a reference: approach, back off, re-approach slowly and set zero, with maximum time and travel limits.

Worked example

A 1.8° motor at 1/16 microstepping and an 8 mm-lead screw gives 3200 / 8 = 400 pulses/mm. Moving 10 mm needs 4000 pulses; 20 mm/s needs 8000 pulses/s. This geometry excludes missed steps, backlash and controller throughput limits.

21Feedback and closed-loop control

Explain P, PI and PID, and evaluate control with measurements instead of random tuning.

You will need: Optional Python 3 and wheel-speed records from the existing driver.

Core concepts

Control starts with error

e = target − measurement. The controller adjusts output using this error. Confirm units and direction first: a positive command with negative measured speed may create positive feedback. Targets, measurements and outputs have different meanings; do not mix rpm and PWM.

The roles of P, I and D

P reacts to present error; too much can cause oscillation. I accumulates error and can reduce steady-state offset, but may wind up. D responds to rate of change and can add damping, but is noise-sensitive. Start wheel-speed learning with P and then PI rather than enabling everything at once.

Sampling, limits and anti-windup

Integration must account for the time interval dt. Physical output is limited, so the integral must not accumulate indefinitely during saturation. Define what happens to integral state on stop, fault and mode changes. Filter delay also affects control behaviour.

Evaluate with metrics

Record rise time, overshoot, steady-state error and disturbance recovery. Change one parameter at a time, beginning with a model or low-risk prototype. An existing driver may already perform PI; do not add a second equivalent loop without understanding its role.

Worked example

For a 100 rpm target and 80 rpm measurement, error is 20 rpm. With Kp = 0.5 command units/rpm, proportional output is 10 command units. It is not necessarily 10 V or 10% PWM: define the output scale.

22Motion and coordinate systems

Convert wheel speed correctly and derive motion from the actual wheel arrangement.

You will need: Your existing car or a chassis drawing.

Core concepts

Define units and positive directions

This course uses body x forward, y left and positive angular velocity ω counterclockwise. Other software may differ. State metres, seconds, radians and rpm explicitly; mm/s and m/s are not interchangeable.

Encoder counts to rim speed

If the output shaft has N counts/revolution and count change ΔC over Δt, rpm = 60ΔC/(NΔt). Wheel rim speed s = πD × rpm/60. Omni B60 nominal diameter is 60 mm and radius 30 mm: do not swap them.

Omni wheels are not mecanum wheels

Wheel speeds depend on wheel location, driven direction and roller geometry. A standard omni wheel’s free-rolling direction differs from its driven direction. Not every four-wheel arrangement can control all three planar degrees of freedom.

Ideal kinematics is not perfect ground motion

For an ideal standard omni wheel at (xi, yi) with unit driven direction ti, si = tix·vx + tiy·vy + (−tix·yi + tiy·xi)ω, and wheel angular speed is si/r. This expression is not a direct mecanum formula. Slip and wheel-size errors require measurement.

Worked example

A 60 mm wheel at 300 rpm has a rim speed of approximately 0.942 m/s. Using its 30 mm radius as the diameter gives half that result. This does not guarantee the whole car travels at 0.942 m/s in every direction.

Core concepts

Start with one load

Validate in layers

28Final project and verification

Core concepts

Choose your project

Options include a sensor-controlled fan, a small angle-positioning mechanism, a linear stage or an improvement to your omni-wheel car. Choose something you can verify with available tools and manageable size and energy.

Test against requirements

Each requirement needs a corresponding test. Mark pass, fail or not tested. A video that looks correct is not a replacement for measurement.

Repeat normal and fault tests

Repeat under the same conditions, then test restart, invalid commands and communication loss. Increase load gradually within verified ratings.

Explain limits and next steps

Being able to identify what you do not know or have not tested is part of independent engineering. Later study may cover control theory, RTOS, mechanisms or circuit design. This course does not require PCB fabrication.

Worked example

An omni-car submission can include a hardware table, power and signal diagrams, one-wheel tests, speed calibration, movement tests and timeout-stop records. Do not claim precise positioning if ground slip has not been measured.
